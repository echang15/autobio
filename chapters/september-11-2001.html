September 2001 · Sophomore year — with reflections from 2026

September 11, 2001

Written in September 2026, ahead of the 25th anniversary, while the memory is still sharp.

I was a sophomore at the University of Delaware, sitting in CISC220, Data Structures, taught by Professor Albright. I always sat in the back — a lazy slacker's seat, the kind you pick specifically so the professor doesn't call on you. It was, as far as I can remember, a completely normal Tuesday morning right up until it wasn't.

I lived in Ray Street dormitory, second floor, room 213 if I remember correctly. The rooms back then were barely furnished — bare concrete walls painted white, bare mattresses on basic wooden bed frames, plain wooden dressers, a futon my roommate had brought from home, and plain wooden desks. Our room shared a bathroom with the room next door — four guys splitting one sink, one shower, one bathroom. This was still the CRT era, so the TV in our room topped out around 20 inches and sat in the corner, away from the computers. My roommate and I were both computer science majors, so we'd each decked out our own PC — big monitor, real office chair — and those got a lot more attention day to day than the TV ever did. This was still Windows 98 Second Edition days. My computer time back then was mostly spent making custom skins for Winamp and tweaking Explorer to get virtual desktops working. I also had my gaming PC overclocked so I could play Starsiege: Tribes and Counter-Strike competitively. In the corner of the room was my Fender Deluxe 112 Plus guitar amp, and there was always at least a guitar or two leaning up against it — a Yamaha Pacifica 112M and a Jackson Kelly.

Near the end of class, one of my classmates spoke up and announced that a plane had hit the first tower. I remember exactly what I thought: total BS. I figured he was making some kind of sick joke, and I didn't think about it again until I got back to my dorm.

I was alone in my dorm room — my roommate was off at a different class. I turned on the TV and saw the smoking tower, and that's when it became real. I was watching live when the second plane hit — I saw the second tower get hit in real time. My heart sank straight to the floor. Complete state of shock, by myself, watching it happen.

It wasn't until I got my dad on the phone that I remembered my cousin Simon was in one of the towers, and the two of us were in shock together over the fact that we couldn't account for him. That's when the shock turned into immediate panic. He's my first cousin on my mother's side — his mom is my mom's sister, and he's a bit older than me. He normally lives in Taiwan, like most of my extended family; I'm the one who ended up in the US. He happened to be in Manhattan on business that day, due to be in one of the lower buildings in the World Trade Center complex. He got out before the towers came down, but leaving the island afterward was its own ordeal — the bridges and all the traffic out of Manhattan were completely deadlocked. My dad drove all the way up to North Jersey to get him. I don't know the exact details of how they connected — whether it was across a bridge or part of the boat evacuations that day — that part is really my dad's story to tell, not mine. But I remember the waiting, and not sleeping, until we knew he was safe. Simon turned out to be completely fine. My dad drove him all the way back to us in Delaware, and he stayed with us for a while before eventually heading home. We were one of the lucky ones — no one in my immediate family lost their life that day.

I didn't sleep that night. At some point I ended up at a diner on Main Street with Andrew, my freshman-year roommate Mike, and Mike's girlfriend at the time (and, much later, his wife) Kate — mostly just to have something to do besides sit with it. A couple of people at a nearby table were talking about the news — trying to make sense of how someone could jump out of a window. My friends noticed how distraught I was getting overhearing that, and one of them walked over and quietly told them about Simon. They stopped talking about it after that.

The Days After

Classes were canceled the day it happened — I don't remember anymore whether that lasted the rest of the week. With nothing else to do, we were basically free the rest of that day, and I remember just being glued to the TV and talking with friends. A couple of them, once they realized I had a cousin who was missing, stayed by my side the entire time. Some of the girls I'd met through Summer College back in 1999, Tina among them, realized I was in distress and stuck with me for a long time, spending a lot of it just sitting with me. Andrew, Mike, and Kate did the same, especially at the diner that night. Andrew and I are still great friends to this day; Mike and I fell out of touch somewhere along the way. I didn't understand how much that would mean to me, or how thankful I'd be for it, until much later in life.

The mood on campus was incredibly somber. Anyone from New York or Long Island got a lot of sympathy and empathy from everyone else that day. The university held a candlelight vigil in the days that followed, and pretty much the entire campus turned out — everyone was in shock together. It changed the tone of that whole school year.

Years Later, on Long Island

This all came flying back to me years later. In late 2004, right after graduating, I moved to New York for my first job out of college, working for Top Line Express in Jamaica, Queens. I'd gotten into street hockey back in college and already knew how to ice skate, so I finally put the two together and joined a beer-league ice hockey club called the Midnight Hockey League, which traveled between rinks all over Long Island. I got assigned to a team called the Blues.

Early on, as I was still meeting teammates and figuring out which rinks we played at, we went to a rink in Freeport, New York. In the lobby, I noticed a couple of hockey jerseys hanging up, sealed in heavy plastic. As I got closer I could see dust and debris on them, and I had a guess at what I was looking at, but I asked a teammate, George, to be sure. He told me those jerseys had been recovered from the rubble of the World Trade Center — they belonged to a hockey team made up of firefighters, and that team had lost most of its roster and had to fold.

I left New York in 2006 to join JPMorgan Chase as a software engineer. I still play hockey to this day, but I unfortunately lost touch with that team and those teammates. Over the two years I spent in that league, meeting team after team, I found the same thing everywhere: every team had been touched by it, and everyone had lost somebody. It was the same at work — right around the JFK cargo area, I never met anyone who hadn't lost a cousin, a friend, or a family member to it. I remember a conversation with a colleague there, Michelle, about the cousin she'd lost — she was still visibly emotional about it, years on. That somber feeling from those years is something I'll never fully shake.

Twenty-Five Years On

The first several anniversaries were incredibly somber. That hasn't really changed — the impact is still extreme — but time has taken some of the rawness off it.

I've been back to Ground Zero and the memorial exactly once in my life. I found it difficult to be there, and I'm not sure I can go back again.

One small, concrete thing that's stayed with me: my dad used to fly a lot for work when I was growing up, and we could run right up to the gate — wait there to watch him take off, then be right at the door again when he landed to hug him. I have very fond memories of that, walking my dad to the gate and being there the moment he came back through it. All of that is gone now, and it's gone for good.

I felt the optimism and hope of the 1990s — that sense of dreaming toward a better tomorrow — come crashing down that day, and it's never really been the same since. I think I'm part of one of the last generations to grow up without constant connectivity to the rest of the world, without being recorded and watched all the time. Looking back, that day feels like it marks the start of a steady rise in the anxiety level of the whole world, one that's never really come back down.
