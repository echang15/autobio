September 2001 · Sophomore year — with reflections from 2026

September 11, 2001

Written in September 2026, ahead of the 25th anniversary, while the memory is still sharp.

I was a sophomore at the University of Delaware, sitting in Data Structures. I always sat in the back — a lazy slacker's seat, the kind you pick specifically so the professor doesn't call on you. It was an entirely ordinary Tuesday morning right up until it wasn't.

Near the end of class, one of my classmates spoke up and announced that a plane had hit the first tower. I remember exactly what I thought: total BS. I figured he was making some kind of sick joke, and I didn't think about it again until I got back to my dorm.

I lived in Ray Street dormitory, second floor, room 213 if I remember correctly. The rooms back then were barely furnished — bare concrete walls painted white, a bare mattress, a futon [TK — how it got there], and plain wooden desks. This was still the CRT era, so the TV in our room topped out around 20 inches and sat in the corner, away from the computers. My roommate and I were both computer science majors, so we'd each decked out our own PC — big monitor, real office chair — and those got a lot more attention day to day than the TV ever did.

I was alone in my dorm room — my roommate was off at a different class. I turned on the TV and saw the smoking tower, and that's when it became real. I was watching live when the second plane hit — I saw the second tower get hit in real time. My heart sank straight to the floor. Complete state of shock, by myself, watching it happen.

Being Asian That Week

Growing up as a minority, I'd already lived through moments like this on TV before and felt the particular dread that comes with it. I remember getting my dad on the phone almost immediately, and the two of us were almost begging — hoping the attackers wouldn't turn out to be Asian, purely because of the racial stereotyping and backlash we knew would follow if they were. That fear, that awareness of how quickly the country's anger could get pointed at people who look like us regardless of the actual facts, has always been a huge lens we grew up watching the world through as immigrants. It was there that day too, underneath everything else.

It wasn't until a little later that I remembered my cousin was in one of the towers. That's when the shock turned into immediate panic. He's my first cousin on my mother's side — his mom is my mom's sister, and he's a bit older than me. He normally lives in Taiwan, like most of my extended family; I'm the one who ended up in the US. He happened to be in Manhattan on business that day, due to be in one of the lower buildings in the World Trade Center complex. He got out before the towers came down, but leaving the island afterward was its own ordeal — the bridges and all the traffic out of Manhattan were completely deadlocked. My dad drove all the way up to North Jersey to get him. I don't know the exact details of how they connected — whether it was across a bridge or part of the boat evacuations that day — that part is really my dad's story to tell, not mine. But I remember the waiting, and not sleeping, until we knew he was safe.

I didn't sleep that night. At some point I ended up at a diner on Main Street with my freshman-year roommate and a friend, mostly just to have something to do besides sit with it. A couple of people at a nearby table were talking about the news — trying to make sense of how someone could jump out of a window. My friends noticed how distraught I was getting overhearing that, and one of them walked over and quietly told them about my cousin. They stopped talking about it after that.

The Days After

Classes were canceled the day it happened — I don't remember anymore whether that lasted the rest of the week. The mood on campus was incredibly somber. Anyone from New York or Long Island got a lot of sympathy and empathy from everyone else that day.

The university held a candlelight vigil in the days that followed. Pretty much the entire campus turned out — everyone was in shock together. Some good friends of mine, people I'd met through Summer College back in 1999, showed up with a lot of empathy and just spent time with me. I really appreciated that. It changed the tone of that whole school year.

Years Later, on Long Island

This all came flying back to me years later. After I graduated in 2004, I ended up working in New York for a small international logistics company in Jamaica, Queens, right by JFK Airport. As a hobby, I picked up beer-league ice hockey — a midnight league that traveled between rinks all over Long Island, playing against other people who just did it for the love of the sport.

Sometime between 2004 and 2006, I was at a rink in Freeport with my team, the Blues, and I noticed a couple of jerseys hanging up from a firefighting team. I asked one of my new teammates what the story was. He got quiet for a second and told me that team used to be made up entirely of firefighters — and that they'd lost most of the entire team on September 11th.

I left New York in 2006 to join JPMorgan Chase as a software engineer. I still play hockey to this day, but I unfortunately lost touch with that team and those teammates. That somber feeling from those years is something I'll never fully shake. Working right around the JFK cargo area for those two years, everyone around me had been touched by that day in some way — I never met anyone there who hadn't lost a cousin, a friend, or a family member to it.

Twenty-Five Years On

The first several anniversaries were incredibly somber. That hasn't really changed — the impact is still extreme — but time has taken some of the rawness off it.

I've been back to Ground Zero and the memorial exactly once in my life. I found it difficult to be there, and I'm not sure I can go back again.

One small, concrete thing that's stayed with me: my dad used to fly a lot for work when I was growing up, and we could run right up to the gate — wait there to watch him take off, then be right at the door again when he landed to hug him. I have very fond memories of that, walking my dad to the gate and being there the moment he came back through it. All of that is gone now, and it's gone for good.

I felt the optimism and hope of the 1990s — that sense of dreaming toward a better tomorrow — come crashing down that day, and it's never really been the same since. I think I'm part of one of the last generations to grow up without constant connectivity to the rest of the world, without being recorded and watched all the time. Looking back, that day feels like it marks the start of a steady rise in the anxiety level of the whole world, one that's never really come back down.
